Julia Adams

abt. 1885 – Island Creek, Pike County, Kentucky

The Matriarch of the Kindred Fortress. Taken into her uncle's household as a young woman, she raised her children inside its shelter for nearly two decades — until a quiet bond was finally made lawful.

Preface: The Architecture of Survival

In the genealogical research involving the Holt, Adams, Tackett, and Mullins families, understanding the structures that protected these lineages is essential. Particularly with Melungeon roots, these families moved in kinship groups, creating layers of security categorized into three concepts:

Concept I
The Kindred Fortress

A social structure of dense, localized biological kin. It maintained insularity through contiguous land and shared identity.

Concept II
The Safety Net

The informal agreements to support one another during crisis, such as absorbing orphans and providing for widows.

Concept III
Sanctuary

The environments that allowed the family to live by their own customs, free from outside interference. This was achieved through legal sanctuary in boundary zones and community sanctuary within the church. Most importantly, the physical terrain of the Appalachian hollows and ridges offered a natural barrier, a sanctuary of distance that made it difficult for census takers, tax collectors, or rival groups to reach the family seat without significant effort.

The Island Creek Sanctuary: A Lifelong Shelter

VITAL FACTS

  • Born: abt. 1885, Magoffin County, Kentucky
  • Parents: Edward "Ned" Adams and Martha Jackson
  • Rebecca's Father: Alexander "Ellick" Mullins
  • Legal Husband: Elijah "Lige" Adams (married 2 April 1921)
  • Children: Rebecca, Lizzie, Virgie, and Kendrick — raised as Adams children
  • Connection to Barrett: Great-grandmother via Romaine Marie Holt

Julia Adams was the daughter of Edward "Ned" Adams and Martha Jackson of Magoffin County. At some point in her youth she came into the household of her uncle, Elijah "Lige" Adams, in Pike County, and from the day she arrived she remained under his roof for the rest of his life. That single fact, more than any other, is the real shape of her story: whatever hardship came to her, she never faced it alone.

In early 1904, at nineteen, Julia gave birth to her first daughter, Rebecca. The child's father was Alexander "Ellick" Mullins, a man roughly twenty years older than Julia who was, at the same time, already married and raising a family of his own nearby. It was never a marriage between Julia and Ellick, and it was never treated as one. In the rigid social structure of the era, an unmarried woman with a child faced immediate social exposure, and for a family of Melungeon descent, that exposure was a genuine threat to their standing in the eyes of the law and their neighbors.

Elijah's household absorbed that risk. His wife at the time, Isabella "Bell" Adams, nearly twenty-five years older than Julia, gave the household the settled, respectable appearance of an established family, and within it, Julia's children, Rebecca, Lizzie, Virgie, and Kendrick, grew up as Adams children in a recognized Adams home. The difficulty later researchers have had untangling these records is, in a real sense, the same shield that once kept the state from untangling them.

Ellick Mullins: The Man Outside the Household

VITAL FACTS

  • Born: 4 March 1855, Pike County, Kentucky
  • Parents: Nelson Mullins and Caroline Matilda "Tildy" Elswick
  • Married: Clarinda Dennis Vanover (February 1878, Wise County, Virginia)
  • Died: 19 February 1917, near Freeling, Dickenson County, Virginia (age 61)
  • Connection to Barrett: Great-grandfather via Rebecca Adams
  • DNA Match: ~12.5% shared with Barrett Holt — the expected figure for a documented great-grandfather and great-grandchild

Alexander "Ellick" Mullins was born 4 March 1855 in Pike County, the son of Nelson Mullins and Caroline Matilda "Tildy" Elswick, a separate Mullins family with its own documented line back through the Elswick and Newsom families of the region. In February 1878, in Wise County, Virginia, he married Clarinda Dennis Vanover, and that marriage, not any union with Julia, was his recognized family. His relationship with Julia ran alongside it, unacknowledged and unrecorded, for years afterward. He died 19 February 1917 near Freeling, in Dickenson County, Virginia, at the age of sixty-one.

⚠ Open Question

Family recollection has also connected Ellick to the name McPeak at some point in his life, though no marriage record or documented household under that name has yet been located, and that thread remains an open question for future research rather than a settled fact.

What is settled is the biological connection itself. Ellick Mullins is documented as Rebecca's father, and the DNA bears that out in exactly the way it should: an estimated 12.5 percent shared match between Barrett Holt and Ellick's own profile, the expected figure for a documented great-grandfather and great-grandchild. That is not a dramatic or exotic finding. It is DNA doing precisely what good paper-trail research predicts it will do, confirming a relationship the records had already established.

The Quiet Resilience of a Mother

While the household at Island Creek provided the framework, Julia's own persistence was its heart. For decades she lived in a liminal space, biologically a mother, but recorded to the outside world under a series of shifting labels, each one a small act of protection rather than a contradiction to explain away.

THE CENSUS TRAIL

1900

A teenage daughter in her own parents' household, Magoffin County — years before Rebecca's birth.

1910

Recorded as Elijah's "aunt"; Rebecca, Lizzie & Virgie appear as Elijah and Bell's own daughters.

1920

The household resettles into niece and nephew — Julia as niece, the children as nephew and niece to Elijah and Bell.

1930

Following Bell's death and her marriage to Elijah, Julia appears plainly as his wife; Kendrick as his son.

1940

His widow, no longer living in the household — it descends to Kendrick, his wife, and their own children.

Read end to end, that progression is not a family struggling to keep its story straight. It is a family adjusting its story, deliberately, to whatever the moment required. She earned her standing through the relentless labor of the mountain hollow, gardening, canning, and running the domestic engine of the farm. She did not choose the Adams name for her children out of convenience; it was the name of the household that had chosen to protect them. By staying within her own kin, she shielded her children from the poor laws that so often targeted fatherless children in mixed-heritage families.

A Bond Made Lawful

Isabella "Bell" Adams died sometime between the 1920 federal census, where she still appears in the household, and April 1921. Shortly after, on 2 April 1921, Elijah and Julia were legally married, formalizing in law a bond that had already sheltered her and her children for close to two decades. He died on 2 September 1932 in Letcher County, Kentucky, and Julia remained his wife until the end.

💍 Married

2 April 1921 — shortly after Bell's death, Elijah and Julia were legally married, formalizing a bond that had already sheltered her and her children for close to two decades.

🕊 Widowed

Elijah died 2 September 1932 in Letcher County, Kentucky. Julia remained his wife until the end.

A marriage between an uncle and his niece reads as unusual today, but it was not a rare arrangement in isolated Appalachian communities, where the same small circle of families intermarried across generations out of necessity as much as choice. It is, in its own way, one more expression of the Kindred Fortress this family built for itself: when the outside world could not be trusted, the family turned inward, again and again, to its own.

The Newsom Reconvergence

The marriage that followed a generation later, between Rebecca Adams and Landon Holt, was not the meeting of two unconnected families. It was the rejoining of a single bloodline that had drifted apart for the better part of a century.

Ellick Mullins's own parents, Nelson Mullins and Caroline Matilda "Tildy" Elswick, carried that bloodline on one side. Tildy Elswick's mother was Nancy Kinney Newsome, daughter of Harrison Newsom and Polly Kinney. On the other side, Harrison Newsom and Polly Kinney's son, John Kinney, was the father of Eliza Jane Kinney, who was the mother of Martha Tackett, whose son was Landon Holt.

THE RECONVERGENCE — ROUGHLY 70 YEARS AFTER THE SPLIT

Two branches of the same Newsom-Kinney root, one running down through a daughter to Ellick Mullins, the other down through a son to Landon Holt, drifted apart for roughly seventy years before Landon Holt married Rebecca Adams, who was Ellick's own daughter. The bloodline found its way back to itself, and the Kindred Fortress, once again, had done its quiet work.

The Enduring Legacy: From Shield to Foundation

The success of a Kindred Fortress is measured by the stability of the generations that follow. Julia's children did not just survive their circumstances; they became the anchors of their own families. Rebecca Adams married Landon Holt, completing the passage from a sheltered, undocumented childhood to a documented, respected adult life, and carrying forward the resilience she had learned on Island Creek.

Final Reflections

Julia's life was a lesson in Melungeon roots and Appalachian strategy. She understood that in a world of shifting jurisdictions and social scrutiny, the surest protection was the one built from kinship itself, an uncle's household that never let her go, a name that shielded her children, and a marriage that eventually made lawful what had sheltered her all along.

We look back at her story, and at the documented line running from Ellick Mullins through Rebecca, through Landon Holt, through Romaine, not only as genealogical facts but as the blueprint of our own survival. Julia Adams remains the matriarch of the high ridges, the woman whose family closed ranks around her and made sure her line would carry on.
